# The Unfinished Game

*Story 01 · canon · ~2,200 words. First in the Marginalia cycle. Tone reference for all Glitch Chess fiction: first-person, warm, curious, no violence — the stakes are quiet ones.*

---

Chess is the oldest program still running, and like all old programs, it has bugs. I know this because my job — eleven dollars an hour, Tuesdays and Thursdays, in the basement of the Ivy City Chess Club — was to scan one hundred years of other people's finished games into a database, forty thousand paper scoresheets soft as moth wings, and because scoresheet number 31,206 records a move that cannot be played.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the whole point of chess and also, it turns out, of reality. On June 14th, 1937, in the fourth round of the Ivy City Summer Invitational, a player named E. Fermata, playing white, wrote down her forty-first move as **Ng1–g3**. If you know chess, you already see the problem. If you don't: knights move in an L. Two squares, then one square sideways, like a horse trying to parallel park. A knight on g1 can reach f3 or h3 or e2. It cannot reach g3, which is directly in front of it, two squares up. That is not a knight move. That is a knight *walking*.

The database rejected it, obviously. The database was the only other one taking my work seriously. `VALIDATION ERROR: illegal move (41. Ng1-g3)`. I checked the scoresheet again. The handwriting was confident. Fountain pen, no hesitation, the 3 finished with a little flourish. And in the margin, in different ink — a looping shorthand I didn't recognize, dense as lace — someone had written a note, and beside the note, a small mark: ✱

Here is the thing about me: I will let a mystery go if it asks politely. This one did not ask politely. This one had a flourish on the 3.

⁂

The game was adjourned. That's the other strange word on the scoresheet — ADJOURNED, stamped diagonally, the way you'd stamp LIBRARY COPY or DECEASED. Mr. Osei, who runs the club and hired me for the digitization grant, explained that adjournment is a dead custom from before computers: if a tournament game ran too long, you'd seal your next move in an envelope, and the game would resume another day. The sealed move kept everything honest. The game slept in the envelope.

"So when did they finish it?"

Mr. Osei looked at the scoresheet for a long time. "They didn't," he said. "That's the Fermata game." And then he said he had to go reset the clocks upstairs, all of them, immediately.

I found the envelope a week later, taped to the back of a drawer in the tournament archive, which I was definitely not paid enough to be organizing. Cream paper, browned at the edges. Sealed. On the front, that same fountain pen: *White's 41st. To be opened at the board, and only at the board.* And below that, in the lace shorthand, another line I couldn't read, and the mark again: ✱

Behind the envelope, filed like it was nothing, like it was a receipt: a catalog card. Old library stock, soft cotton paper. It said:

**CONTINUATIONS — see the MARGINALIA**
*between Fourth & Fifth · ask for the night clerk*
*bring both halves*

I live on Fourth. I have walked to that club two hundred times. There is nothing between Fourth and Fifth except a gap where the buildings don't quite agree with each other — a slot of alley you'd miss if you blinked, cobbled in alternating pale and dark stones.

I had blinked, apparently. For six years.

⁂

The alley was eleven cobbles wide. I counted, because by then I was counting everything. Pale, dark, pale, dark. Halfway down, between one dark cobble and the next pale one — and I want to be precise here, because this is the part I still have trouble with — there was a bookshop *in the seam*. Not behind a door in the wall. In the gap between two stones, the way a bookmark is in the gap between two pages, except the bookmark was forty feet tall and had bay windows and a brass mail slot and warm amber light coming through the glass, and the sign said THE MARGINALIA in letters that had been repainted so many times they were slightly 3D.

Inside it smelled like every library you have ever loved plus something underneath — warm dust, or the ghost of toast. The shelves went up too far and leaned in directions that shelves don't. The books were not organized by author. Little brass plates on the shelf-ends said things like *KING'S GAMBITS, DECLINED FOR GOOD REASONS* and *ENDGAMES, THEORETICAL* and *ENDGAMES, ACTUAL* and, at the very back, one long dim aisle labeled *UNFINISHED*.

The night clerk found me there. I never heard her coming, which I would later learn is a professional qualification. She was small and gray and wore a cardigan that looked knitted from fog, and her glasses were perfectly round, and she turned her head toward me a little further than heads turn.

"Both halves," she said, and held out her hand.

I gave her the scoresheet and the envelope. She read the margins — read the lace shorthand, easy as a menu — and for a second the round glasses went bright, twin amber moons, and she said, quietly, "Oh, *Elena*." The way you say the name of a friend who owes you a letter.

⁂

Here is what the night clerk told me, over tea, in the reading nook the shop keeps inside a hollowed rook the size of a garden shed. I am giving you the short version. The long version had digressions about paper acidity.

Chess is not a game. Chess is a *diagnostic*. The world was written a very long time ago by builders nobody has names for, and they left one small program running to check the work: sixty-four squares, thirty-two processes, rules that fit on two pages. Every finished game is a completed test. Every completed test tells the world, in a voice too small to hear, *still working, still working, still working*. This is why chess exists in every country. This is why it never dies out. The world keeps it running the way you keep breathing.

You can guess the next part. An unfinished game is a test that never returns. A hole. Small — a game's worth of hole, whatever that is. The world routes around it, mostly. But holes wear at their edges, the way a dropped stitch wears at a sweater, and after enough decades the wear becomes visible, if you know how to look. A parking meter that never runs out. A door everyone forgets is a door.

Or: a stationery shop on Fourth Avenue with blank signage, that has been "closed for renovation" my entire life, that I have never once seen anyone enter, exit, or mention. A shop I could not photograph — I tried, on the walk over, twelve times; every picture came back glare.

"Fermata and Daughters, Fine Paper," the night clerk said. "Elena's family shop. She sealed her forty-first move on a Monday, and on Tuesday her family moved west, all of them, very suddenly — paper trouble, money trouble, ordinary trouble. She wrote to the club for years asking to resume by post. It's against the rules." She refilled my tea. "She died at ninety-six. The obituary said she kept a chess set by the window, arranged in a position no one recognized."

The game had been asleep in its envelope for eighty-nine years. The hole had been wearing at Fourth Avenue for eighty-nine years.

"So someone has to finish it," I said.

"Someone has to finish it *properly*," said the clerk. "At the board. Two players. And the position, when it wakes up, will be — " she paused, choosing the word with tongs — " *irregular*. It has been dreaming a long time. Positions dream." She looked at me over the round glasses. "You'll sit for Signal. Elena's side. I'll sit for Static — someone must, and the shop owes her that."

"I'm rated eleven hundred," I said. "On a good day. You should get literally anyone else."

"Skill finishes games quickly," the clerk said. "It doesn't finish them *well*. Besides —" and here she reached under the tea table and set something between us, "— you won't be unarmed."

It was a paper packet about the size of a deck of cards, cream-colored, tied with red thread. On the front, printed in careful worn type: **FIELD KIT №1 — SIXTEEN PRESSED GLITCHES — THE MARGINALIA — *for repairs*.**

⁂

You should understand what a pressed glitch is, because you may own some one day and I don't want you to be casual with them. When the world stutters — when a knight arrives somewhere it couldn't have gone, when a square flickers, when a pawn moves with a memory it shouldn't have — an archivist writes the stutter down in tracery, the lace shorthand, which does not describe the glitch so much as *hold* it, the way sheet music holds a song. Pressed flat. Filed. Sixteen to a packet, because sixteen is half of thirty-two, and repairs are always done in halves.

Play one at a board where reality is thin, and the glitch runs again.

We set up the position from scoresheet 31,206 on the shop's oldest table, under a lamp with a green glass shade. Forty moves of 1937 chess, reconstructed piece by piece. The clerk dealt us three cards each from the field kit, face up — "House rules. Repairs are played open." My three: a card called ROLLBACK, dense with tracery; a card called FIREWALL, with a drawing of a little amber wall; and a card with a small pawn in a red scarf standing very straight in front of a great blank wave, titled FERMATA, and I said, "Wait," and the clerk said, "Later. Open the envelope."

The seal broke like a sigh. Inside, in fountain pen, with the flourish: *41. Ng1–g3.* And folded around the notation, thin as onion skin: a seventeenth card. Old. Hand-pressed. The tracery on it was the same hand as the margins.

"She sealed a glitch in with her move," the clerk said softly. "She saw the knight walk, in 1937, in the fourth round of the Summer Invitational, and instead of telling the arbiter, she *wrote it down and kept it*." She shook her head slowly, with the deepest respect. "Glitchwright instincts. Don't tell the Order I said that."

So White's forty-first move was a knight, walking, one square then another, straight up the file like a small stubborn person going home. The lamp dimmed. Three squares on the old board flickered — I saw it, I was watching, d4 and d5 and one square I've chosen not to name. And the game woke up.

⁂

I will not narrate eighty-nine-years-asleep chess to you move by move, though I could; I remember every move, which is itself unusual for me. I will tell you it was not like club chess. The position was irregular, as promised. It dreamed at us. The clerk played Static the way weather plays a coastline, and I lost a piece every few moves, and every time I lost a piece I drew a card — house rules, repairs run on loss — until my side of the table looked like a very strange solitaire and hers looked like a museum.

I played ROLLBACK once and unlost a bishop, and the clerk nodded like a teacher when you finally use the right formula. I built the little amber FIREWALL on the square I'm not naming, and something that had been leaning on the position all evening quietly stopped leaning.

And at the end — down to almost nothing, Elena's last pawn and my king against the clerk's patient armada — I looked at the seventeenth card, Elena's own pressed glitch, which I had been saving without admitting I was saving it, and I read the one line of tracery I had, by then, learned to read, because the clerk had written it out for me on a napkin at move fifty:

*A pawn is a queen who has agreed, for now, to walk.*

I played it. The pawn crossed the last rank. And the pawn *remembered*.

I want to be precise: I did not win. Promotion, check, a scramble, and the clerk's rook fell on my king like closing time. Static won in ninety-one moves, fifty-four years after White's death, at 1:34 in the morning, at a table between two cobblestones. The clerk extended her hand over the board and I shook it, and the moment our hands met, the whole shop went quiet in a way I can only describe as a held breath being *released* — the way a room sounds when a refrigerator you never noticed shuts off.

"The Lattice doesn't care who wins," the night clerk said, already fetching the ledger. "It cares that it ends."

⁂

On the walk home, on Fourth, the stationery shop had its lights on.

Signage: FERMATA & DAUGHTERS, FINE PAPER, gold on green, not blank at all, obviously never blank, what a strange thing for me to have thought. Through the window, a young woman was setting out boxes of fountain pen cartridges and — I stopped on the sidewalk and laughed out loud — a display of small red scarves. My phone photographed it on the first try. The shop opens at nine. Everyone in the neighborhood mentions it constantly, fondly, as if they always have. I have checked. I am the only one keeping receipts.

I still scan on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Eleven dollars an hour, plus tea. But now I read the margins first, and I keep a tally in the back of my notebook that the grant office will never see: forty thousand scoresheets, of which — so far — two hundred and fourteen are stamped ADJOURNED, sealed moves lost or waiting, each one a small hole wearing at the edges of somewhere.

Two hundred and fourteen unfinished games. The night clerk says one of them — she won't say which, the round glasses go bright and she changes the subject — was left adjourned by the builders themselves.

I've bought a better scanner. I've learned tracery through the letter K. And on my desk, in a cream packet tied with red thread, I keep a field kit of my own, sixteen pressed glitches, *for repairs* — because the world is old, and the tests must run, and somewhere out there a pawn is walking toward the last rank of something, remembering.
