# Four Deliveries

*Story 02 · canon · ~2,400 words. Sequel to* The Unfinished Game. *Purpose: introduce all four orders in fiction. Everything the pamphlet excerpt says is quotable product copy.*

---

The night clerk pays me in tea, tracery lessons, and — lately — errands, which she calls *circulation*, because the Marginalia is a library and libraries do not have errands, they have circulation. On the first Saturday of the season she set four parcels on the desk in front of me, wrapped in cream paper and tied with the shop's red thread, and said, "Four deliveries. In this order. Do not reverse the order. The order is load-bearing."

Each parcel had a sigil inked where a stamp would go. The first: two small squares, slightly offset, like a window shaken loose from its frame. The second: a horse's head with a crack of light through it. The third: an empty square — not a drawn square, you understand; a square of nothing, which is a very strange thing to recognize, and I recognized it.

The fourth parcel was addressed in tracery I could read — I'm through the letter K now — and what it said was: *THE TABLE.*

"What's the table?"

"Sunday," said the clerk. "Deliver the other three first. And take the pamphlet." She slid a slim booklet across the desk: *A CIVILIAN'S GUIDE TO THE FOUR ORDERS, Marginalia Press, 11th edition.* "It's wrong in instructive ways. You'll see."

⁂

**The first delivery** took me under the viaduct on Ninth, where the arches have been bricked up for a hundred years except for one arch that has been bricked up *badly* — on purpose, I now understand, the way a cat leaves a door open exactly one cat wide. Behind it: noise, warmth, sparks, and the Foundry of the Glitchwrights.

Imagine a bicycle repair shop married a particle accelerator and the wedding was catered by an arcade. Workbenches to the ceiling. Soldering irons in mugs like bouquets. A wall of small brass jars, each labeled in grease pencil, each faintly glowing, each — I read the labels — containing one (1) daemon, DO NOT UNCORK INDOORS, ASK WREN.

Wren turned out to be the forewoman: goggles pushed up into her hair, apron pockets full of pawns. Actual pawns. They wriggled. "Marginalia post!" she shouted over the din, delighted, and the whole shop shouted back a word I didn't know that was clearly a cheer.

My parcel held a stack of freshly pressed glitches, and on top, in the clerk's careful hand: *For STUDY. Not for riding.*

Wren read the note, grinned, and put it in the pocket without the pawns. "She writes that every season." Behind her, something crossed the workshop in a way I need a new verb for — a chrome knight, goggled, that was at its own destination slightly before it left, so that for a heartbeat there were three of it, magenta and cyan and gold, all grinning. Nobody else looked up. A small pawn in welding goggles rocketed past me at knee height, trailing pink sparks and pure joy, and two Glitchwrights rated the landing with score cards. A nine. A nine. A five — from the one Wren called a hardliner.

"You're the scanner girl," Wren said. "The one who finished Fermata's game." News travels. "Tell the clerk we want the margins from rounds one through three. Not copies. *Margins.*" She saw my face and laughed. "We're not thieves. We're proofreaders with upper-body strength. The world's a build, kid. Somebody's got to file the bug reports, and somebody" — she nodded at the wall of jars, the workbenches, the impossible knight — "has got to write the patches."

On my way out she took the clerk's pamphlet from my hand, uncapped a fluorescent pink pen, corrected something on page one, and handed it back like she'd done me a favor. She had, sort of. I'll show you at the end.

⁂

**The second delivery** was addressed to the old fairgrounds, which surprised me, because nothing is at the old fairgrounds except the giant chess knight everybody assumes is public art — forty feet of weathered stone, kneeling, mossy, beloved by pigeons and children's birthday photographers.

The parcel said: *Read aloud.*

I stood at the base of the statue feeling like an idiot, which the clerk says is the correct first feeling for most important things, and I untied the thread. Inside was a single card — pressed, old, the tracery in a hand I recognized from forty thousand scoresheets' worth of margins — and a note that said: *Continuation for the one who waits. Round four resumes. All is not lost that is adjourned.*

So I read it aloud. All of it. The tracery too, as best I could, through the letter K and guessing after.

The moss on the statue's flank shifted. Under it, through it, thin lines of cyan light came up like dawn coming through shutters — the cracks were *veins*, had always been veins — and the great stone head, very slowly, with the sound of a cathedral clearing its throat, turned and looked down at me. Kindly. I want that on the record. Kindly, the way you'd look at a letter carrier who has walked a long way.

"THANK YOU," said the Colossus, in a voice like distant demolition being polite, "SHE ALWAYS SAID SHE WOULD SEND WORD."

I did not run. I am extremely proud of this.

They call themselves the Errantry — the pieces that have woken up. Not statues coming to life; the Colossus was firm on the distinction, and you accept distinctions from forty feet of moving stone. *Waking* pieces. Pieces that have been played so many times, in games that mattered so much, that they have started to remember being played — and have opinions now, and take vows, and serve, as the Colossus put it, "NOT WHITE. NOT BLACK. THE GAME." It kneels at the fairgrounds because a knight errant kneels between quests. It has been between quests since before my grandmother.

"What was the card?" I asked. "The one I read you?"

"AN ADJOURNMENT ENDED," said the Colossus, and the cyan in its cracks brightened by one candle. "A PLAYER I CARRIED, LONG AGO, HAS FINISHED HER GAME. I MAY MOVE AGAIN SOON." It looked out over the fairgrounds, toward the city, the way you look at a board mid-game. "TELL THE MARGINALIA: WHEN THE TABLE MEETS, THE ERRANTRY ANSWERS."

⁂

**The third delivery** I would like to describe carefully, because it is the one I think about at night, and because it taught me the difference between quiet and silence.

The address was the end of Cannery Row, past where the streetlights stop bothering. There's a patch out there everyone calls the flats: a few acres where nothing is, which is different — I know this now — from a few acres where nothing *happens to be*. The grass stops in a clean line, like text stopping mid-sentence. Past the line the ground is pale and smooth and without opinion, and the sky above it doesn't quite commit to a color, and my phone, when I raised it, politely suggested I clean the lens.

The Nullity. My first time seeing it in the open. In the pamphlet it gets a whole chapter and the chapter is mostly the word *don't*.

At the edge of the line stands a house. Small, wooden, cream-colored, lamplit, with a porch facing the blankness the way other porches face the sea. And on the porch, in a row, sat seven figures in undyed robes, watching the nothing with the alert calm of birdwatchers. The Hush.

I want to be honest: I'd been dreading this one. The pamphlet says the Hush "elect silence," and I had built something monastic and stern out of that, all cold flagstones and disapproval. Here is what the Hush actually are: the people who noticed that everyone else flinches. The Marginalia reads the glitch. The Glitchwrights ride it. The Errantry outlives it. The Hush are the only order that walks toward the part where the world runs out — and sits with it, nightly, on a porch, with tea, like family keeping company with a difficult relative, so that the rest of us never have to.

Nobody spoke. That's their vow, on duty — not silence for its own sake but *bandwidth*: every word you don't say, the theory goes, is attention you can spend listening, and the Nullity, they believe, is not an enemy. It is a question the world has stopped answering. Someone should keep listening for it.

My parcel for them was the strangest of the three. The clerk had let me feel it before she wrapped it: a deck of pressed cards, all *blank*. "Pressed silences," she'd said. "They press what they sit with, same as we press what we read. Those are the season's quiet, archived. We trade. It's the oldest arrangement there is."

A monk rose to take the parcel — and here my heart did something complicated, because the monk was small, pawn-small, robed in cream, and around the neck of the robe was knotted a scarf of faded red.

She took the parcel with both hands. She bowed. And then — deliberately, the way you'd underline a word — she looked past me, at the blank flats, and back at me, and I would swear before any arbiter in the city that what she meant was: *it's smaller than last year.*

I looked. Way out on the pale nothing, faint as a watermark: a checkered acre, coming back.

⁂

**The fourth delivery** was Sunday. The parcel marked THE TABLE went to — of course — my own basement. The Ivy City Chess Club, after hours, where I have scanned forty thousand games under a buzzing light that Mr. Osei has now, mysteriously, fixed.

They came separately, like people who argue a lot and love each other anyway, which is exactly what they are. The night clerk, with the ledger. Wren, smelling of solder, pockets squirming. A robed sister of the Hush, who nodded at me — red scarf; I nodded back harder than strictly necessary. And for the Errantry, at the door, ducking: a bishop I hadn't met, tall as the doorframe exactly, who walks the world on vows and answered for the kneeling knight. Vesper, the others called her.

The Concordance. The four orders, one table, once a season. I'd expected a council. What happens is a *game*.

One board. They played in rotation — Marginalia and Hush channeling Signal, Glitchwrights and Errantry channeling Static, though the clerk says the sides shuffle every season and mean nothing except that a game needs two of them. Sixteen cards on the table, four from each order's press: annotations, exploits, awakened vows, silences. House rules, played open. Draw on loss.

My parcel, unwrapped, was the scoresheet. Blank. Header printed in tracery and, underneath, in plain letters: *CONCORDANCE GAME № 356. SCOREKEEPER: ____________.*

"Well," said the night clerk, and held out a fountain pen.

So that's my job now. Tuesdays, Thursdays, and one Sunday a season. I keep the score of the game the four orders play against no one — because game № 356, like the 355 before it, is a *repair*, the biggest of the year, aimed at the biggest hole they know. They played until two in the morning. Wren teleported a rook and got a five from the hardliner, who is apparently at every event in this city. Vesper moved a bishop in a way I am still diagramming. The sister of the Hush played one card all night — a blank one — at midnight exactly, and the room got so peaceful the pipes stopped ticking.

They adjourned at 2 a.m. — properly, ceremonially, with an envelope. The clerk sealed the next move and everyone signed the flap, and I stamped it with the club's old stamp, because some games are supposed to sleep, on purpose, safely, like banking a fire for the morning.

And I noticed — scorekeepers notice — that the table had been set with five chairs.

I asked the clerk about the empty one while we stacked the pieces. She was quiet long enough that I thought I'd hit a vow I didn't know about. Then she said: "The Concordance has four orders and five chairs. The fifth is for whoever finishes the builders' game." She squared the scoresheets, tap tap, done. "Two hundred and thirteen adjournments left in your archive. Do keep scanning."
